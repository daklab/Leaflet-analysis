\section*{Methods}\label{Methods}
\subsection*{Single-Cell Smart-seq2 Data Processing}
We processed publicly available datasets to generate inputs for the LeafletFA model training and evaluation (\textbf{Fig.} \ref{fig:FIGURE1}A). All measurements were taken from distinct individual cells or nuclei, and no samples were measured repeatedly. For mouse data, the genome reference GENCODE vM19 (GRCm38) was used for all alignments and annotations. For human data, GENCODE v45 (GRCh38) was used to capture annotated splice junctions. Four primary data sources were utilized to cover a wide range of tissues and species:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Tabula Muris Senis (TMS):} Single-cell Smart-seq2 BAM files from the Tabula Muris Senis consortium \cite{Tabula_Muris_Consortium2020-ga} were obtained from the Chan Zuckerberg Biohub AWS S3 bucket:\\
{\footnotesize \url{s3://czb-tabula-muris-senis/}}\\
Processed count matrices for total gene expression were used for gene expression analysis. In Tabula Muris Senis, age coincides with sequencing campaign and library format: no sequencing plate contains cells from more than one age, the 3-month animals were sequenced in a different campaign and library format from the 18- and 24-month animals, and all 24-month animals are male. Age effects therefore cannot be separated from batch in this dataset, and age is not analyzed.

\item \textbf{Allen Brain Atlas - Mouse:} Smart-seq2 based single-nuclei RNA-seq data (ssv4) from the Allen Institute for Brain Science \cite{mouse-allen-brain-ssv4} were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO accession: GSE185862) and the Allen Brain Map portal:
{\footnotesize \url{https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq/mouse-whole-cortex-and-hippocampus-smart-seq}}\\
Processed count matrices, specifically exon-only counts or adjusted intron and exon counts, were used for gene expression analysis. Of 72,704 Allen cells with junction calls, 9,400 were omitted because of a file-layout difference between two processing runs. They were similar to the included cells in class, subclass, region and donor composition (Supplementary Table SX).

\item \textbf{Tabula Sapiens:} Single-cell Smart-seq2 data from the Tabula Sapiens consortium \cite{Tabula_Sapiens,TabulaSapienV2} were obtained from:
{\footnotesize \url{https://tabula-sapiens-portal.ds.czbiohub.org/}}\\

\item \textbf{Allen Brain Atlas - Human:} Human brain single-nuclei Smart-seq2 data from the Allen Institute were obtained from the Allen Brain Map portal:\\
{\footnotesize \url{https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq/human-multiple-cortical-areas-smart-seq}}

\subsection*{Gene Expression Normalization and Integration}
\label{sec:GeneExpressionNMF}
To enable the joint analysis of single-cell and single-nuclei datasets, we implemented a multi-step normalization and integration pipeline. Following best practices for Smart-seq2 data analysis \cite{Phipson2017-fw}, we normalized raw gene counts to account for transcript length bias by dividing each gene's counts by its mean transcript length and then multiplying by the overall median transcript length across all genes. For the Allen Brain nuclei data, which contains intronic reads, we applied the same normalization to intronic counts using mean intron lengths. After length normalization, counts underwent library size normalization and a \texttt{log1p} transformation.

Cells were retained if they carried at least 200 detected genes and at least 5 detected splice junctions, and were removed as likely doublets if their detected-gene or detected-junction count exceeded the dataset median by more than 5 median absolute deviations. Cells lacking a cell-type annotation were removed. Genes were restricted to protein-coding and lncRNA biotypes by GTF annotation. The 21-month TMS age group (728 cells) was excluded from all analyses as too small for the age contrasts.

For gene-expression embeddings we trained Linear scVI \cite{linear_scvi} ($K=20$ latent dimensions, batch-aware) on the length-normalized counts of the top 20{,}000 highly variable genes together with all annotated RNA-binding-protein and aging-associated genes. \subsection*{Alternative Splicing Quantification and ATSE Mapping}
Splice junctions were extracted from BAM files using regtools \cite{Regtools} and processed using our ATSEmapper pipeline (\url{https://github.com/daklab/LeafletFA-utils/tree/main/leafletfa_utils/atsemapper}). Each identified splice junction was validated using species-specific reference genome FASTA files to confirm the presence of canonical SS motifs (GT-AG, GC-AG, or AT-AC). GTF annotation files were used to classify each SS as either annotated (matching known exon boundaries) or novel, allowing us to retain high-confidence novel splicing events while filtering out potential alignment artifacts.

A splice graph is constructed for each gene where nodes represent unique SS and edges represent the observed junctions connecting them. An Alternative Transcript Structure Event (ATSE), analogous to an "intron cluster" in LeafCutter \cite{Leafcutter}, is defined as a set of two or more junctions that are interconnected through common SS, forming a single connected component within the gene's splice graph. ATSEs capture various forms of alternative splicing including cassette exons, alternative 5'/3' SS, and alternative first/last exon usage (\textbf{Fig.} \ref{fig:FIGURE1}B).

Junction coordinates were grouped into ATSEs within each species separately, with mouse and human datasets processed independently to maintain species-specific SS coordinates and annotations. Junctions were retained if supported by at least 100 reads across at least 10 cells, with intron length between 50 bp and 500{,}000 bp; both annotated and unannotated junctions passing the motif check were kept. After cell QC, junctions detected in fewer than 25 cells were removed and cells with fewer than 500 detected junctions were dropped; ATSEs were then re-derived on the surviving junctions by recomputing connected components of the splice graph, so that no ATSE carries a junction that was filtered out. Junctions that no longer belonged to any multi-junction ATSE were discarded. The number of reads supporting each junction, and the total across the ATSE it belongs to, were stored per cell in sparse \texttt{AnnData}\cite{Anndata} objects. The final mouse atlas comprises 132{,}706 cells, 79{,}768 junctions and 24{,}475 ATSEs (10{,}677 with two junctions; mean 3.26 junctions per ATSE); 13.8\% of (cell, junction) entries carry any reads.

\subsection*{LeafletFA Dirichlet-Multinomial Factor Model}\label{sec:LeafletFAMethod}

LeafletFA models, for each cell $c$ and each ATSE $e$, the vector of junction read counts $\mathbf{y}_c^{(e)} = (y_{cj})_{j \in e}$ conditional on the ATSE total $N_{ce} = \sum_{j\in e} y_{cj}$. Each cell is a mixture $\phi_{c:}$ over $K$ latent splicing programs, and each program $k$ has a usage composition $\psi_{k,\cdot}^{(e)}$ over the junctions of every ATSE. The expected usage of junction $j$ in cell $c$ is
$$\rho_{cj} = \sum_{k=1}^K \phi_{ck}\,\psi_{kj}.$$
Because every $\psi_{k,\cdot}^{(e)}$ is a composition and $\phi_{c:}$ is a mixture, $\boldsymbol{\rho}_c^{(e)}$ is itself a composition over each ATSE's junctions -- the model cannot place more than the ATSE's total splicing mass on its junctions.

\paragraph{Programs.} Junction usage within an ATSE is parameterised on the simplex through a softmax of unconstrained logits,
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{kj} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) & \text{for } k = 1,\ldots,K;\ j = 1,\ldots,J \\
    \psi_{kj} &= \frac{\exp \eta_{kj}}{\sum_{j' \in e(j)} \exp \eta_{kj'}} & \text{for each ATSE } e,
\end{align*}
so that $\sum_{j \in e} \psi_{kj} = 1$ for every program and every ATSE. This replaces the independent per-junction Beta usage ratios of an earlier version of the model, under which the $m$ junctions of an ATSE were treated as $m$ separate binomial trials that need not sum to one; only $m-1$ of them are free, and treating them as $m$ over-counts the evidence by $m/(m-1)$ -- a factor of two for the 44\% of atlas ATSEs with two junctions.

\paragraph{Cell loadings.} 
\begin{align*}
    \alpha_\pi &\sim \text{Gamma}(1, 1) \\
    \pi \mid \alpha_\pi &\sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_\pi\mathbf{1}/K) \\
    \phi_{c:} \mid \pi &\sim \text{Dirichlet}(\delta\,\pi), \qquad \delta = K,
\end{align*}
for each cell $c = 1, \ldots, C$. The Dirichlet concentration $\delta$ is fixed at $K$, which sets the average effective concentration $\delta\pi_k$ to 1. When $\delta$ was instead learned it fell to values placing every $\delta\pi_k$ between 0.19 and 0.37: a Dirichlet with all parameters below 1 is sparsity-inducing and drives each cell into a corner of the simplex, and under the sparse per-cell evidence of Smart-seq2 data that corner choice was found to be dominated by noise, collapsing the loadings.

\paragraph{Likelihood.} The junction counts of an ATSE are drawn jointly,
$$\mathbf{y}_c^{(e)} \mid N_{ce}, \boldsymbol{\rho}_c^{(e)}, \gamma_e \;\sim\; \text{DirichletMultinomial}\!\left(N_{ce},\; \gamma_e\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_c^{(e)}\right),$$
with one concentration $\gamma_e \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 0.1)$ per ATSE. Under the simplex parameterisation the Dirichlet's total pseudo-count $\sum_{j\in e} \gamma_e \rho_{cj}$ equals $\gamma_e$ exactly. At $m = 2$ this is the Beta-Binomial; for larger $m$ it is its exact multivariate generalisation, and it is the same likelihood used by LeafCutter's differential-splicing model, verified term for term against that implementation.

\paragraph{The concentration is an effective sample size.} $\gamma_e$ is the number of independent draws the model believes a cell contributes to an event. Smart-seq2 libraries carry no unique molecular identifiers, so a cell's reads over an ATSE are PCR copies of a small number of cDNA molecules rather than independent observations. Reads on a junction are largely PCR duplicates: even deeply covered events carry only a few effective molecules (Supplementary Note 1). We floor $\gamma_e$ at 2, which matches the model-implied number of effective molecules per event (2.15 in Tabula Muris Senis and 2.17 in Allen Brain; \cite{SpliceVI2026}, Supplementary Fig.~S1). The number measured directly from the read data, as the median over all observed events, is lower, about 1.2 (1.19 in Tabula Muris Senis and 1.26 in Allen Brain; same figure), so the floor permits less overdispersion than the data show. The floor is a modeling choice, not a measurement. It is binding for most events: in the fitted atlas model ($K = 20$, seed 0) 75\% of the 24{,}475 per-ATSE concentrations sit at exactly 2 (median 2.0, 99th percentile 20.4), so for those events the overdispersion is set by the floor rather than estimated from the event's own data. Left unconstrained, the fitted concentration collapses to a median of 0.52, i.e. fewer than one independent molecule per event, which is not physical, and the loadings $\phi$ collapse with it. This treatment does not by itself distinguish technical duplication from genuine single-cell isoform commitment; under either, a low concentration is the correct description of the per-cell evidence.

\subsection*{Model Fitting}\label{Methods:Minibatch}

We fit LeafletFA by stochastic variational inference in Pyro \cite{pyro}, maximising the evidence lower bound (ELBO) with a mean-field Gaussian variational family over the unconstrained parameters (Pyro's \texttt{AutoNormal}). Cells are conditionally independent given the global parameters, so training uses a single Pyro subsampling plate: at each step a minibatch of 8{,}192 cells is drawn, the local loadings $\phi$ for those cells and the global parameters ($\eta$, $\pi$, $\gamma$) are updated together, and the local terms of the ELBO are rescaled by $C/8{,}192$ so that every step is an unbiased estimate of the full objective. Full-atlas fits ran for 16{,}200 steps at learning rate 0.025 with exponential decay to $0.1\times$ over the run (Adam, gradient-norm clipping at 10). The per-dataset fits at $K = 30$ (TMS only, mouse Allen brain only, and human Allen brain; each under 60{,}000 cells) used learning rate 0.015, because at 0.025 the larger rank diverged repeatedly on these smaller datasets; all other settings were unchanged. Non-finite losses -- which occurred on every atlas fit before this schedule was fixed -- trigger a rollback to the last checkpointed parameter state rather than terminating the run. Because the ELBO on this data reaches within noise of its final value in the first $\sim$50 steps and its tail slope is not informative, convergence was judged on the between-program contrast of $\psi$ (the mean pairwise distance between programs' compositions), which continues to move for thousands of steps and is what the model is expected to learn.

We trained models at $K \in \{10, 20, 30\}$ with three random initialisations each; $K = 20$ is used for all reported analyses and the other ranks serve as robustness checks (the posterior differential-splicing call set at $K=20$ overlaps the $K=30$ set at Jaccard 0.71 with per-contrast rank correlation of effect sizes 0.97, and the $K=10$ set at 0.52; the likelihood-ratio and LeafCutter arms do not depend on $K$). On held-out cells, $K = 20$ performed as well as $K = 10$ and better than $K = 30$ in cell-type separation (LeafletFA validation, check 2). $K$ acts as an upper bound rather than a quantity to tune: the sparse prior on the loadings (most $\delta\pi_k < 1$) leaves programs the data do not need empty, so only 5--8 of the 20 programs are used, which is why $K = 10$ and $K = 20$ perform the same. Initialisation is from the prior; no data-derived initialisation was required once the single-plate training was in place. Programs are numbered for presentation by their fitted mixing weight $\pi$, so that SP1 is the program active in the largest share of cells; the numbering is applied per fit and programs are matched across fits only through their compositions (below).

\subsection*{Differential Splicing Between Cell Groups}
\label{sec:DS_group}
LeafletFA tests differential splicing between two groups of cells $A$ and $B$ at the level of the ATSE, using the fitted model's posterior. All quantities below are computed per ATSE and per posterior draw; $S = 300$ draws are used throughout.

\paragraph{Statistic.} For posterior draw $s$, let $\phi^{(s)}_{c:}$ and $\psi^{(s)}_{k,\cdot}$ be draws from the variational posterior of the cell loadings and program compositions ($\phi$ through its stick-breaking parameterisation, $\psi$ through the within-ATSE softmax, so both remain on the simplex). The difference in mean loading between the groups is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi^{(s)}_k = \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{c\in A}\phi^{(s)}_{ck} - \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{c\in B}\phi^{(s)}_{ck},
\end{equation}
and because $\rho$ is linear in $\phi$, the difference in expected usage of junction $j$ is $\Delta_j^{(s)} = \sum_k \Delta\phi^{(s)}_k\,\psi^{(s)}_{kj}$. Both groups' loadings are drawn from the posterior rather than fixed at their means; the loadings carry the between-group biology, so treating them as known would understate the spread of every downstream quantity. The effect size for ATSE $e$ is the total variation distance between the two groups' predicted compositions,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TV}^{(s)}_e = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in e}\left|\Delta_j^{(s)}\right| \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
the fraction of the event's splicing mass that moves between the groups. Because every program's composition sums to one over the ATSE's junctions, $\sum_{j\in e}\Delta_j^{(s)} = 0$: mass lost by one junction is gained by the others, and the factor $\tfrac12$ counts each moved unit once. For a two-junction ATSE, $\mathrm{TV}_e = |\Delta\mathrm{PSI}|$; for a cassette exon (inclusion junctions J1, J2 and skip junction J3) with the inclusion junctions moving together, $\mathrm{TV}_e = |\Delta\mathrm{PSI}_{\mathrm{skip}}|$. As a worked example with $K=2$ programs whose compositions over (J1, J2, J3) are $(0.40, 0.40, 0.20)$ and $(0.25, 0.25, 0.50)$, and group means $\bar\phi_A = (0.8, 0.2)$, $\bar\phi_B = (0.3, 0.7)$ so that $\Delta\phi = (0.5, -0.5)$: $\Delta_{J1} = \Delta_{J2} = 0.5\cdot0.40 - 0.5\cdot0.25 = 0.075$ and $\Delta_{J3} = 0.5\cdot0.20 - 0.5\cdot0.50 = -0.15$, giving $\mathrm{TV}_e = \tfrac12(0.075 + 0.075 + 0.15) = 0.15$ -- 15\% of the event's mass moved from the skip junction to the inclusion junctions. Since $\sum_j \psi_{kj} = 1$, $\mathrm{TV}_e \le \tfrac12\|\Delta\bar\phi\|_1$ for every ATSE (here $0.5$); an event reaches that bound only if the programs favoured by the two groups use disjoint junctions. We report the posterior mean of $\mathrm{TV}_e$ as the effect estimate.

\paragraph{Calling.} An ATSE is called differentially spliced when the posterior probability that its effect exceeds a stated minimum,
\begin{equation}
    P_e = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S \mathbf{1}\!\left(\mathrm{TV}^{(s)}_e > \delta\right),
\end{equation}
is high. $\delta$ is the effect size of interest, not a significance level: it says how large a change we are willing to call, and the separate false-discovery control below says how sure we are. Multiplicity is controlled through the posterior expected false discovery proportion: ranking ATSEs by $P_e$ descending, the expected FDP among the top $n$ is $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\le n}(1-P_{(i)})$, and the called set is the largest $n$ for which this is at most $\alpha = 0.05$. This is the Bayesian FDR of the earlier version of the method, applied to a per-ATSE rather than per-junction statistic.

\paragraph{Choosing $\delta$ from the model's own resolution.} $\delta$ has to sit above the value $\mathrm{TV}_e$ takes when nothing differs -- the model's resolution floor -- and that floor scales with $|\Delta\phi|$, so it cannot be read from a null that collapses the loadings (permuting cell labels between the groups drives $\Delta\phi \to 0$ and the test degenerates to the point null ``$\Delta \ne 0$'', which is essentially always true). We measure the floor with permuted negative-control ATSEs: a random 10\% of ATSEs have their per-cell count vectors permuted across cells (each cell keeps its own $N_{ce}$, so depth and composition are preserved and only the association between cell identity and usage is destroyed), the model is refit, and those events are null by construction while the untouched 90\% keep the loadings at their true magnitude. Their $\mathrm{TV}_e$ distribution under a real contrast is the floor at the right scale. The construction was validated on simulations, where truly null events are known: permuted and truly-null events had indistinguishable $\mathrm{TV}$ distributions (median ratio 0.93--1.02 across contrasts and effect sizes). On the mouse atlas the 99th percentile of the floor over eight cell-type contrasts was 0.13; we use $\delta = 0.15$. On simulations at this $\delta$ the realised FDP is 1.2--2.0 times nominal with power 0.46--0.90 for effect sizes $\ge 0.35$; at $\delta = 0.05$ the same test is 5--6 times anti-conservative, because $\delta$ then lies below the posterior standard deviation of $\psi$ itself (0.02--0.09 per junction). Since fitted loadings vary in scale between initialisations, we report as called the ATSEs called in at least two of three independently seeded fits; the rank ordering of $\mathrm{TV}_e$ across seeds is stable (Spearman $\rho \approx 0.90$ within contrast).

\paragraph{Coverage gate and data support.} Because $\phi$ and $\psi$ are defined for every cell and every event, the test returns a value even where the two groups contain almost no reads for the event; there it reports the model's prior, not the data. We therefore restrict calls to (contrast, ATSE) pairs with at least 25 covered cells in each group. A second, distinct issue is that $\psi_{k\cdot}$ is a property of the \emph{program}, shared by every cell that loads on program $k$ across all tissues. At an event where cell types elsewhere in the atlas genuinely differ, the fitted $\psi$ of two programs can be far apart, and $\mathrm{TV}_e$ for a contrast whose groups load on those programs is then large even if the cells in that contrast do not follow the programs at that event. To make this visible we compare, for every call, the model's group-mean composition $\bar\phi_A\psi$ against the pooled observed junction usage of the same cells, and report separately the calls where the observed pooled composition itself differs by $\mathrm{TV} \ge \delta$ (data-supported) and where it does not (program-level inference). This is a limitation of any model with a fixed number of shared programs, and the two classes are reported rather than merged.

\paragraph{What the test can and cannot see.} Because $\sum_{j\in e}\psi_{kj} = 1$ for every program, $\mathrm{TV}_e \le \tfrac12\|\Delta\bar\phi\|_1$ for every ATSE: the largest effect the posterior test can report in a contrast is bounded by how differently the two groups use the programs, and empirically the 99th percentile of $\mathrm{TV}_e$ across a contrast's ATSEs is $0.34\,\|\Delta\bar\phi\|_1 - 0.04$ ($r = 0.95$ over 70 contrasts). Two cell types that share programs -- marrow B cells and monocytes, or skin basal cells and keratinocytes -- therefore receive no posterior call at any ATSE regardless of the observed usage difference; 29 of the 70 contrasts are of this kind. The test asks whether two groups differ in \emph{program} usage at an event, and it is reported as such alongside the label-based tests, which do not carry this restriction.

\subsection*{Likelihood-Ratio Test for the Comparison with LeafCutter}
\label{sec:DS_LRT}
For a like-for-like comparison with LeafCutter we also test each ATSE with LeafCutter's own inference -- a Dirichlet-multinomial generalised linear model \cite{Leafcutter} -- but with individual cells as the unit rather than pseudobulk replicates. Under $H_0$ every cell in the contrast shares one composition, $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_e = \gamma_e\,\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{b})$; under $H_1$ the composition depends on a binary cell-type indicator $x_c$, $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ce} = \gamma_e\,\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{b} + x_c\,\mathbf{B})$, with the last junction's coefficient fixed at zero for identifiability. The statistic $2(\ell_1 - \ell_0)$ is referred to $\chi^2_{m-1}$. Because Wilks' approximation is exactly what could fail at $m = 2$ and low coverage, we do not assume it: calibration is measured on a matched permutation null (below), and on simulations against known truth.

\paragraph{Comparison design.} LeafCutter was run on pseudobulk replicates constructed by aggregating cells within each cell type into seven or eight replicates per group, the realistic design for Smart-seq2 differential splicing. LeafletFA's LRT was run on the same contrasts using every individual cell. Each method's calibration was measured on its own permutation null -- for LeafCutter, cell-type labels shuffled within a cell type; for LeafletFA, cells of a single cell type split at random into two arms. Because a within-type split can carry residual donor structure, LeafletFA's null is contaminated by real signal and its inflation ratio is an upper bound; the comparison is therefore conservative with respect to LeafletFA's calibration and, since it forces a tighter threshold, conservative against LeafletFA's power. Methods were compared at matched \emph{realised} false discovery proportion: each was thresholded where its own null delivered an FDP of 0.05 (LeafCutter $q \le 1.39\times10^{-3}$, LeafletFA $q \le 3.16\times10^{-2}$), since comparing counts at a common nominal $q$ would score miscalibration as sensitivity. Where the posterior effect-size test is compared with LeafCutter, the comparison is restricted to events whose effect size, as measured by LeafCutter's own pseudobulk estimates ($\tfrac12\sum_j|\Delta\mathrm{PSI}_j|$), exceeds $\delta$, so that both methods are asked the same question.

\subsection*{Program Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
Programs from independently seeded fits, and from fits at different $K$, were matched by the Hungarian algorithm on the cosine similarity of their compositions $\psi$ (never on $\phi$, whose per-cell values are the poorly determined direction), after centering each fit's $\psi$ on its $\pi$-weighted mean composition. Centring is necessary: every program shares most of each junction's usage, so uncentered compositions are about 0.98 similar for every program pair and cannot separate a reproducible program from one that is not. A program is counted as reproduced when it is used by at least one cell type (maximum cell-type mean activity $\geq 0.05$) and its matched centered cosine is at least 0.8 in both other seeds; this is a stated cutoff, as the distribution of matched similarities is a smooth gradient rather than bimodal. On the atlas fit at $K = 20$ four programs are reproduced (hippocampal and cortical excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, and keratinocyte/basal epithelium). For these, a consensus composition is defined as the mean of the matched programs' $\psi$ across the three seeds, renormalized within each ATSE, and statements about individual programs are restricted to them. All other analyses use the programs jointly, as the basis of the per-cell representation $\phi$ and of the differential-splicing test, and do not depend on the identity of any single program; the per-cell representation is itself stable across seeds (in three-seed fits on TMS and on mouse Allen Brain training cells, the cell-type $k$-nearest-neighbour accuracy of held-out cells varies by at most 0.025 between seeds). Cross-$K$ matching is one-sided from the smaller rank into the larger. We also asked whether programs that fail this criterion are recovered at a different resolution, as splits or merges of programs in the other fits (non-negative least-squares regression of a program's centered composition on up to three programs of another fit, $R^2 \geq 0.8$). This rescued no program in Tabula Muris Senis and only Allen Brain neuron programs, where one fit's mixed excitatory and inhibitory program was split into separate programs in the others. Programs that were not reproduced usually had a counterpart used by the same cell types in each fit (median correlation of cell-type activity profiles 0.81--0.92) but with a different junction composition (median overlap of the top 50 junctions 2--19\%). Cell-type usage of programs is therefore reproducible, while their junction composition is reproducible only for the programs that meet the criterion above. As an example of a program that does not meet the threshold, a program used mainly by endothelial and smooth-muscle cells has a counterpart used by the same cell types in each of the three seeded fits, but their compositions agree only partly (centered cosine 0.54--0.65), and in one fit the program closest in composition is used by epithelial cells instead (Supplementary Fig.~SX); statements about it are therefore restricted to what the fits share.

\subsection*{Simulating Junction Usage Ratios Across Cell Types}\label{sec:simulation_method}

Our simulations are based on observed ATSE counts and predefined cell types from real TMS heart cells (6{,}986 cells, 4{,}268 three-junction ATSEs) so that the sparsity of read coverage is that of real data: every cell's total read count over each ATSE is taken verbatim from its real value. Every 3-junction ATSE is randomly assigned a positive (has DS across cell states) or negative label (no DS), 30\% positive (\textbf{Fig.} \ref{fig:SUPP_FIGURE1}A). The three splice junctions belonging to a given ATSE are sorted into the two corresponding to exon inclusion (\textbf{J1} and \textbf{J2}) and the one corresponding to exon exclusion (\textbf{J3}). Cells are assigned to $K=5$ latent states by their annotated cell type. For negative ATSEs we sample one shared $\psi^{J3}$ from Beta$(0.5,0.5)$ (hyperparameters chosen to emulate the known bimodality of splicing patterns \cite{NirYosefCoverage}) for all states. For positive ATSEs we sample an independent $\psi^{J3,\mathrm{ind}}_k \sim \text{Beta}(0.5,0.5)$ for each state $k$ and set $\psi^{J3}_k = (1-\epsilon)\,\psi^{J3} + \epsilon\,\psi^{J3,\mathrm{ind}}_k$, where the effect parameter $\epsilon \in \{0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.35, 0.50, 1.00\}$ controls how far states are pulled apart. We then set \( \psi^{J1}_k = \psi^{J2}_k = (1-\psi^{J3}_k)/2 \) for each $k$, so the true total-variation effect between two states is exactly $|\Delta\psi^{J3}|$. Junction counts for a cell are drawn jointly for the whole ATSE, $\mathbf{q} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\gamma\,\boldsymbol{\psi})$ then $\mathbf{y} \sim \text{Multinomial}(N, \mathbf{q})$, so the counts sum to the observed total exactly; the concentration $\gamma$ was 50 in the main simulations and 2 -- the effective sample size measured on the atlas -- in the matched-overdispersion set. Differential splicing is scored against the contrast-specific truth $|\psi^{J3}_p - \psi^{J3}_q|$ for the two states compared, not against the generative label, since two of five states can share a value on a flagged event. LeafletFA was fit with the same model and settings as the atlas (simplex $\psi$, per-ATSE concentration floored at 2, $\delta = K$) on a shorter schedule suited to the 6{,}986-cell data: 3{,}000 steps, minibatches of 2{,}048 cells, learning rate 0.05 with exponential decay to $0.5\times$. It was fit at the generating rank $K=5$ with three model seeds per simulation, and additionally at $K=3$ and $K=10$ to measure the cost of a misspecified rank; all simulation figures report mean $\pm$ SD across seeds. LeafCutter was run on the same simulations by splitting the cells of each state at random into six pseudobulk replicates (six versus six per state pair); since simulated cells carry no donor structure this is the most favourable setting for LeafCutter's dispersion estimate. Program recovery is scored by matching learned to true programs on the correlation of their compositions and by the silhouette of the learned $\phi$ against the true state labels; detection of differential ATSEs from the learned $\psi$ alone is scored as the AUROC and AUPRC of $\max_k\hat\psi_{k}-\min_k\hat\psi_{k}$ against the generative label.

\subsection*{Held-Out Imputation}
\label{sec:imputation}
To evaluate imputation on cells the model had never seen, the atlas was split 70/30 (92{,}894 / 39{,}812 cells) and LeafletFA was refit on the 70\% alone. Reads in the held-out 30\% were removed at 10, 25, 50, 75, 90 and 99\% of their original depth (and, for the sanity analysis, at 0, 10, 50 and 90\%). For each held-out cell at each depth the loading $\phi_c$ was inferred by a variational E-step with every global parameter ($\psi$, $\pi$, $\gamma$) frozen at its trained value; the imputed usage of junction $j$ in cell $c$ is the Dirichlet-multinomial posterior mean $(\gamma_e\rho_{cj} + y_{cj}) / (\gamma_e + N_{ce})$, which shrinks the cell's observed ratio toward its program-predicted composition $\rho_{cj} = \sum_k\phi_{ck}\psi_{kj}$ by an amount set by the event's effective sample size; where the cell has no reads at the event it equals $\rho_{cj}$. Imputed values are scored against each cell's full-coverage usage on (cell, junction) entries covered at full depth with at least five reads, restricted to the highly variable junctions selected on the training split and expanded to whole ATSEs. Because the usage level differs far more between junctions than between cells of one junction, a correlation pooled over all entries is dominated by the between-junction spread and is high for any method that recovers each junction's average (a per-junction mean scores 0.86); we therefore report the median within-junction correlation across cells (does the method rank cells?) and the median within-cell correlation across junctions, over all entries and separately over entries whose reads were all removed, where every method must genuinely impute. Baselines are non-negative matrix factorisation at the same rank on the observed ratios with unobserved entries mean-imputed and the per-junction mean over observed cells; a per-cell-type mean was not used as a competitor because it consumes labels the model does not see. On the 39,812 held-out cells the within-junction correlation of the pure prior $\rho_{cj}$ is 0.09 at every depth, the per-junction mean is 0 by construction, and on entries whose reads were all removed the posterior mean reverts to the prior (0.04--0.08 at 10--90\% removed); NMF at the same rank scores 0.03--0.05 on those entries. Where the cell retains reads the posterior mean holds 0.95, 0.90 and 0.66 within-junction correlation at 10, 50 and 90\% removed, against 0.86, 0.80 and 0.51 for a per-junction mean updated by the same reads and 0.10, 0.09 and 0.07 for NMF. LeafletFA denoises junction usage at single-cell resolution by pooling information across all cells (through $\psi$) and all ATSEs (through $\phi$): each cell's usage is estimated from its program membership, shrunk toward its own reads where they exist, and reported with a posterior uncertainty (below). Because a single cell's observed usage at one ATSE rests on $\sim$2 effective molecules, per-cell estimates cannot be scored against per-cell observations -- the observation, not the estimate, sets this floor, and a per-cell-type mean that uses the labels scores 0.04 on the same entries -- so per-cell estimates are validated where the observation noise averages out: per cell type and per group, which is also where the differential-splicing test and program analyses use $\rho_{cj}$. Per-cell imputation uncertainty was taken as the posterior standard deviation of $\rho_{cj}$ under 200 joint draws from $q(\phi)q(\psi)$; on the \textit{Fgfr2} IIIb/IIIc event it tracks the between-seed spread of the imputed value across cells (Spearman 0.74 over cells, 0.76 over cell types) at a median ratio of 1.2, i.e.\ slightly conservative.

\subsection*{RBP Aging Mediation Analysis}

To identify potential regulatory drivers of age-associated splicing changes, we conducted a systematic mediation analysis focusing on a curated list of 269 RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). We hypothesized that chronological age ($X$) influences the expression of specific RBPs ($M$), which in turn modulates the activity of observed SPs ($Y$) (\textbf{Fig.} \ref{fig:FIGURE5}B). This analysis was performed independently for each tissue-cell type pair.

We utilized the \texttt{mediation\_analysis} function from the \texttt{pingouin} Python package. For each cell, the mediator variable ($M$) was defined as the denoised expression level of a given RBP, obtained from the linear scVI-normalized expression layer to minimize technical noise and dropout effects. The outcome variable ($Y$) was the cell-specific SP activity ($\phi$) inferred by LeafletFA. The significance of the indirect effect (the mediation effect) was determined using bootstrapping with 500 iterations to estimate 95\% confidence intervals and p-values.

To account for multiple hypothesis testing across the suite of RBPs and SPs, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction to the indirect effect p-values within each cell type. An RBP was considered a significant mediator if the FDR-corrected p-value was less than 0.05. The Aging Mediation Coefficient was defined as the standardized coefficient of the indirect effect, representing the magnitude and direction of the aging signal propagated through RBP expression to the splicing program activity (\textbf{Fig.} \ref{fig:FIGURE5}C).

\subsection*{Cross-species Splicing Conservation Analysis}
For the revised analysis the mouse--human junction map was regenerated, scripted end-to-end, against the 2026 model inputs (mouse atlas, GRCm38; human Allen Brain cortex, GRCh38/GENCODE v45): junction coordinates are taken directly from the trained models' junction sets, genes are restricted to Ensembl one-to-one orthologs, mouse junction ends are lifted with \texttt{liftOver} chains (mm10ToHg38) via \texttt{pyliftover}, and a mouse junction is matched to a human junction of the same gene and lifted strand when both ends agree within 100 bp, with duplicate mappings resolved to one-to-one by total coordinate distance. This yields 20{,}583 matched junction pairs (49\% coordinate-exact) covering 10{,}571 ATSE pairs, of which 5{,}804 have at least two matched junctions and are used for composition-level comparisons. Program conservation is then assessed on the models trained separately per species (identical recipe, $K$ and seeds): for every pair of mouse and human programs, the Pearson correlation of $\psi$ over matched junctions, computed on the \emph{deviation} of each program's composition from its species' $\pi$-weighted mean composition -- the raw correlation is dominated by the shared junction-average usage (mean-vs-mean baseline $r = 0.64$) and would overstate conservation -- with programs paired by the Hungarian assignment and the spread reported over all nine seed pairs. The 2025 procedure below describes the original mapping, whose sequence-validation step is retained as an optional stricter filter.

To evaluate conservation of alternative splicing between human and mouse, we filtered ATSEs in each species to retain only splice junctions derived from genes with one-to-one orthologs, as defined by Ensembl. To focus on simpler splicing topologies, we included only ATSEs involving five or fewer junctions. All annotations were derived from GENCODE vM19 (mouse) and v45 (human), aligned to the GRCm38 (mm10) and GRCh38 (hg38) genome assemblies, respectively.

First, splice junction coordinates were extracted from ATSE definitions and exported to BED format for cross-species comparison. To assess coordinate-level conservation, we used the UCSC \texttt{liftOver} tool to map junction coordinates between assemblies in both directions: mouse junctions were lifted from mm10 to hg38, and human junctions were lifted from hg38 to mm10. The lifted coordinates were then compared against all annotated junctions in the target species to identify possible matches within a ±100 bp window. This proximity-based approach captures biologically equivalent splicing events that may have different coordinates due to evolutionary changes, annotation differences between assemblies, or minor assembly variations.

For sequence-based validation of coordinate matches, we extracted ±50 bp of exonic context flanking donor and acceptor sites from both the lifted junction coordinates and their matched target junction coordinates in the respective genomes. We computed pairwise sequence similarity scores using global alignment and evaluated splice motif conservation (GT–AG, GC–AG). Junctions were classified as \textit{conserved} ($\geq$80\% sequence similarity with conserved motifs), \textit{sequence\_only} ($\geq$80\% similarity without motif conservation), \textit{motifs\_only} (conserved motifs with <80\% similarity), or \textit{diverged} (neither criterion met).

To ensure one-to-one relationships, duplicate mappings were resolved by selecting the junction pair with the highest sequence similarity and lowest coordinate distance. This two-step approach of proximity-based coordinate matching followed by sequence validation identifies high-confidence orthologous splice junctions, ensuring that coordinate proximity represents genuine conservation of the same biological splicing event across species.
